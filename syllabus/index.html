Syllabus

Overview

The course

This course prepares students to practice their discipline alongside AI agents, treating an agent as a component of engineering workflows. Students establish the scientific basis (neural networks, how LLMs differ from deterministic numerical methods), operate a modern professional scientific software stack (version control, code review, type checking, testing, CI), and perform key operations by hand before delegating, so they can verify and debug what the agent does. Each student builds a structured input layer driving a process simulation and an agent that operates on it: proposing design changes, invoking the simulator as a deterministic tool, reading results, and iterating toward an improved design under explicit constraints and human-in-the-loop checkpoints, targeting yield, energy efficiency, and/or selectivity. Every graduate ships at least one working agentic system that solves a real engineering or research problem in their own field. The course is offered by the Department of Chemical Engineering and open across the Technion, while case studies are adapted to other domains as needed. The course uses open-source and free-tier tooling wherever possible.

Before Week 1

Prerequisites

  • 02340128 Introduction to Computing (Python).
  • 00540330 Simulation Laboratory, for students of the Department of Chemical Engineering. Students of other departments take an equivalent simulation course, approved in advance.
  • Basic knowledge and proficiency of Python.
  • Python at reading fluency. You will read agent-written Python and review a diff; you will not be asked to write it from scratch.

Verifying that the prerequisites are met is the student's responsibility.

Structure

Weekly rhythm

Every week has the same shape, so a student never wonders what a session is for.

The lecture: 4 blocks, 120 minutes.
Block Min Content
Field update 10 What changed in the field in the last week. Open content.
Concept 70 The week's idea, taught.
Live build 30 The lecturer advances their own agent with this week's capability in front of the class, including what goes wrong.
Your turn 10 Exactly what this week adds to your project.
Total 120
The exercise session: 3 blocks, 120 minutes.
Block Min Content
Standup 10 Three students, two minutes each, screen-shared: where they are, what broke.
Your project 100 This week's capability applied to your own project.
Commit and disclose 10 Commit, push, one line in docs/disclosure.md; milestone checks run in CI.
Total 120
  • Standup slots are assigned, not volunteered, and announced a week ahead. Three students present each week.
  • The Success and Failure gallery has a session of its own in Weeks 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11 — the week after each milestone falls due — and Week 13's showcase carries the last. Successes are named and credited; failures are anonymised.
  • check-sandbox.sh is demonstrated by a teaching assistant in Week 2 and handed to you to run yourself. It is not a weekly re-check and not wired into CI.
  • Week 12's lecture content is open by design; its exercise half, like Week 11's, is project build and the red-team exchange. Open lecture time is about 15% of lecture minutes.

Project

The project spine

Every student carries one project through the semester: an Approach C system, a language model reasoning over a tested deterministic ground in a propose, run, read, adjust loop. It is delivered in six milestones on a two-week beat, and CI checks each one — the named artefact either exists at that week's tag or it does not.

The question it is graded on is where you draw the line between what the model reasons about and what the ground computes. You are asked it four times: a guess in Week 1, a qualification in Week 6, an implemented boundary in Week 8 and a defended claim in the Week 12 report.

Details and the milestone dates are on the Project page.

The live defence. It is held in Week 13 and carries the following conditions, stated here in advance.

  • Not every student is called. The syllabus's own words are "not all, random selection": the students who demonstrate and defend in Week 13 are drawn at random.
  • The questions are the six published in Week 1, listed on the Project page.
  • Attendance in Week 13 is mandatory for every student. Anyone may be called, so everyone must arrive ready to defend their project, unscripted, before the whole course staff.
  • The defence confirms or revises the grade your artefacts already earned: a student who cannot account for their own work has the product, report and demo regraded against what they can actually account for.
